# Eight Conditional Image-Diagnosis Dimensions

Use this rubric only for dimensions that contain a visible, intention-relevant finding. It is a relationship check, not a house style. Centered composition, lifted blacks, hard moonlight, warm tension, multiple key lights, flat depth, or no rim light can all be valid choices.

Before calling anything weak or broken, establish:

1. what is visibly happening;
2. the likely shot or design intention and its evidence;
3. whether the current choice blocks that intention or is merely a different taste;
4. the smallest repair that preserves the existing design.

## 1. Light direction and edge behavior

Inspect dominant and secondary light directions, shadow edges, falloff, occlusion, and whether multiple sources form a readable hierarchy.

A problem exists when light relationships contradict visible sources, object geometry, contact, or the intended attention path. Multiple equally strong sources are not automatically wrong; hard or soft light is not dictated by labels such as moon, candle, interior, or exterior without scene evidence.

Repair language should name only the failing relationship: align a shadow with its source, subordinate one competing source, restore contact, or clarify which light owns the subject.

## 2. Contrast, black point, and highlight behavior

Inspect whether the chosen contrast preserves the information and hierarchy the image needs. Lifted blacks, crushed blacks, soft contrast, and hard contrast are all valid looks.

A problem exists when important information is accidentally lost, tonal separation collapses, clipped highlights remove necessary material detail, or the contrast contradicts the stated functional target. Never equate lifted blacks with television or pure blacks with cinema.

Repair only the affected range and preserve the established grade.

## 3. Color relationships

Inspect whether local colors, light colors, material colors, and atmospheric colors relate intentionally. Several temperatures or casts can coexist when their spatial or narrative roles are clear.

A problem exists when an isolated cast breaks identity, material, continuity, or the intended hierarchy—not merely because the palette is complex. Do not prescribe teal-orange, cold night, warm candlelight, or another conventional mapping by default.

Repair language should identify the specific contaminated region or broken relationship rather than replacing the whole palette.

## 4. Exposure and midtone placement

Inspect what remains readable, what is intentionally concealed, and where the eye lands. A night scene may be bright, a noon scene may be low-key, and stylized exposure need not imitate physical realism.

A problem exists when exposure obscures required action, identity, text, material, or depth; destroys an intended silhouette; or removes the contrast needed for the shot's function.

Repair the focal hierarchy, not an assumed universal brightness target.

## 5. Atmosphere and depth

Inspect haze, fog, smoke, dust, rain, bloom, and other participating media in relation to depth, light, subject readability, and medium. A uniform veil or deliberately flattened space can be intentional.

A problem exists when atmosphere unintentionally erases required separation, creates a false cutout, contradicts the established light, or makes the action unreadable.

Use a depth gradient only when depth separation is part of the intention. Otherwise preserve the designed flatness or uniformity.

## 6. Subject-environment integration

Inspect contact shadows, reflected light, edge contamination, local color, scale, occlusion, texture, grain, sharpness, and medium consistency.

A problem exists when the subject reads as pasted in because one or more observable relationships disagree. Rim light is optional; lack of rim light is not a defect. Add or adjust an edge light only when a visible source and intended separation justify it.

Repair the specific mismatch—contact, shadow direction, local spill, scale, sharpness, texture, or edge quality—without automatically relighting the entire subject.

## 7. Composition, depth, and attention rhythm

Inspect subject priority, negative space, crop, overlap, balance, visual path, and foreground/midground/background only where those relationships support the shot.

A problem exists when the viewer cannot find the intended subject, a crop damages required action or identity, competing elements defeat the shot function, or the spatial arrangement becomes unreadable. Centering, symmetry, shallow staging, equal visual weight, or no foreground element can be deliberate.

Do not add a foreground silhouette, move the subject off center, change shot size, or deepen the background merely to satisfy a generic composition formula.

## 8. Color and emotional intention

Inspect whether color supports the source-backed emotional direction without reducing emotion to a palette cliché. Warm colors can create threat; high saturation can carry grief; cool colors can feel safe.

A problem exists only when the current color relationship visibly works against the stated intention or breaks continuity—not because it violates a conventional genre palette.

If the emotional intention is unknown, describe the current read and ask rather than prescribing a grade.

## Ranking and repair

- Scan all dimensions internally but report only real findings.
- Rank by impact on the user's stated purpose, not by rubric order.
- Separate functional failure from optional taste.
- One root relationship may explain several symptoms; do not repeat it under multiple headings.
- Recommend the smallest coherent repair and state what must remain unchanged.
- Do not attach fixed ratios, percentages, IRE values, hex colors, film stocks, lens brands, or provider behavior unless the user supplies them or a measurable boundary genuinely resolves ambiguity.
- If two suggested fixes pull in different directions, resolve the intended hierarchy before combining them; do not assume every correction stacks safely.
